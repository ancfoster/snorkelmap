{# Example Description #}

Starney Bay is located in the St Abbs Head Nature Reserve. A fairly steep path leads down from the main footpath at the top of the cliffs down to a rocky pebble beach.

St. Abbs is known for its visibilty, offering better visiblity than other locations along the East Coast. At Starbey Bay you will find a variety of different underwater environments and a variety of cold water wildlife. Suitable for beginners with some previous snorkelling experience. This location is best snorkelled in spring, sumemr and autumn when conditions are calmer.

{# Example Entry Point Description#}

The main entry is via the stone steps beside the harbour wall at St Abbs village. The steps are steep, and the lower three are covered in weed and very slippery at anything below half tide, so it is worth sitting down to put fins on.

An alternative entry over large boulders is possible 100 metres to the north, but only in a flat calm and only above half tide. Exit is at the same steps, there is a rope handrail on the left hand side.

{# Reef — Examples #}

A reef is a shallow marine structure built from rock, coral, or other hard substrates that creates habitat for marine life. In reef science, a reef can be described both as an ecological type (the community of organisms living there) and an environment type (the physical conditions and setting that shape how that community functions).

There are 15–30+ distinct reef types, but here are a few examples you may have encountered.

{# Rocky — Examples #}
  • Rocky Shore: Bedrock running from the shoreline into the water.
  • Boulders: Large loose rocks, often with gullies and swim-throughs between them.
  • Drop Off: A sudden steep increase in depth, sometimes near vertical.
  • Pinnacles: Isolated rock towers rising from deeper water.
{# Seabed & Substrate — Examples #}
  • Sandy Bottom: Fine, pale sediment. Often good for rays and flatfish.
  • Volcanic Sand: Dark grey or black sand, common around volcanic islands.
  • Pebble / Shingle: Rounded stones, usually where there is wave action.
  • Rubble: Broken coral or rock fragments, often at the base of a reef.
  • Mud: Soft silt that clouds easily when disturbed.
{# Vegetated — Examples #}
  • Kelp Forest: Tall brown seaweed with a canopy at or near the surface. Common around the UK, Ireland and other cool coasts.
  • Seagrass Meadow: Grass-like flowering plants rooted in sand or mud.
  • Mangroves: Salt-tolerant trees with submerged root systems, an important nursery habitat.
  • Posidonia Meadow: Mediterranean seagrass forming dense, deep meadows.
{# Structures — Examples #}
  • Wreck: A sunken vessel, whole or broken up.
  • Artificial Reef Structure: Deliberately placed blocks, modules or sunken material now colonised by marine life.
  • Pier / Jetty Piling: Vertical supports, usually heavily encrusted and good for small life.
  • Tetrapods: Interlocking concrete sea defence blocks.
{# Geological — Examples #}
  • Sea Cave: A cave cut into rock by wave action, entered from the water.
  • Sea Arch / Swim-through: A gap or tunnel through rock that you can swim through.
  • Lava / Volcanic Formation: Solidified lava flows, tubes and columns.
  • Flooded Limestone / Karst: Cenotes, sinkholes and flooded limestone systems.
{# Fish — What do these categories mean? #}
  • Colourful reef fish: Angelfish, butterflyfish, wrasse, damselfish. Coold water examples include cuckoo wrasse and ballan wrasse.
  • Schooling fish: Sardines, sand eels, mackerel, jacks. These are fish which move in a group of many individuals of the same species.
  • Flatfish: Plaice, flounder, dab, halibut, turbot, sole. Lying flat on the seabed, often half buried.
  • Large pelagic fish: Open water fish such as tuna, marlin and sunfish.
  • Big coastal fish: Wolffish, bass, pollack, cod, grouper.
  • Medium fish: Mullet, parrotfish, bream, mackerel, salmon and sea trout.
  • Small & juvenile fish: Blennies, gobies, sticklebacks and the young of larger species.

{# Crustaceans — Examples #}
{# Molluscs — Examples #}
  • Scallops: Sitting on or recessed into sand and gravel.
{# Echinoderms — Examples #}
  • Starfish & Brittle Stars: Common starfish, sunstars, and dense brittle star beds.
  • Urchins: Spiny, often wedged into rock. See the hazards step if they are numerous.
{# Cnidarians — What do these categories mean? #}
  • Hard corals: Brain coral, staghorn coral, table coral.
  • Soft corals: Sea fans, gorgonians, dead man's fingers.
  • Sea anemones: Plumose, jewel, beadlet and carpet anemones.
  • Hydroids: Fine feathery growths on rock and weed. Some sting.
{# Reptiles — Examples #}
  • Turtles: Green, hawksbill and loggerhead are the usual snorkelling encounters.
{# Cephalopods — What do these categories mean? #}
  • Octopus: Eight-armed molluscs in rocky crevices.
  • Cuttlefish: Remarkable colour-changing ability.
  • Squid: Fast-moving, often seen near the surface at dusk.
{# Sessile Life — Examples #}
  • Sea Sponges: Encrusting or branching, filtering water.
  • Tube Worms & Feather Dusters: Fans that retract instantly when you get close.
  • Tunicates: Sea squirts, often in colonies on rock and wreckage.
{# Birds — Examples #}
  • Diving seabirds: Guillemots, shags, cormorants and gannets seen underwater.
{# Currents — Examples #}
  • Rip current: A band of churned water moving seaward. Move parallel to shore to escape.
  • Tidal current: A sustained directional flow that changes with the tide.
  • Surge: A rhythmic push and pull from swell reflecting off rock.
{# Marine Life Hazards — Examples #}
  • Jellyfish: Check the water before entering. Lion's mane and Portuguese man o' war both sting badly.
  • Stinging coral: Fire coral causes an immediate burning rash.
  • Biting fish: Triggerfish defend nesting sites aggressively and will bite fins and legs. Grey triggerfish are increasingly seen in UK and Irish waters.
  • Territorial mammals: Bull seals during the breeding season.
  • Spiny creatures: Sea urchins, weever fish and scorpionfish underfoot or in the sand.
{# Washing & Changing — Example #}

Toilets and changing at the harbour car park (April to October). Showers at the dive centre on the main street.

{# Food & Drink — Example #}

Cafe at the top of the harbour, open weekends out of season. Nearest supermarket is in Eyemouth, about 10 minutes by car.

{# Safety & Emergency — Example #}

No lifeguard cover. Public rescue equipment (lifebuoy) on the harbour wall by the steps. Nearest A&E is Borders General Hospital, roughly 45 minutes away.
